## An uneasy thought

While I was job hunting recently, I noticed something that made me a bit uneasy.

I’ve started watching how AI agents think: how they ask follow-up questions, break a problem down and reason step by step. Watching them, I realised that a good data scientist should already work like a good agent. Ask questions, break the problem down, reason it through, and find the problem the other person really has.

So if agents are learning to do this, where is my value?

That’s what I want to think through here. What really has to change in your mindset from junior to senior, and which parts of the job AI really can’t reach in the near term.

## Listen first

Here is the fact people most often miss: a data scientist’s job is mainly to help other people solve problems with data. Building models is only one part of that.

So the first step is to really understand the other person. More than on the surface. They should feel that you get their situation, and that your questions hit the thing they care about. When they feel understood, they start to trust you.

This is exactly what agents can’t do well yet. An agent can break down a problem that is already clearly defined. But sitting across from someone who can’t yet say what they want, and asking questions until the real problem comes out, is still work for people.

## Build trust

The second step is building trust.

Trust doesn’t come from throwing out a perfect solution the moment you hear the problem. More often it comes from showing the other person that you care, that you take it seriously, and that you can carry it.

A mature, steady person doesn’t panic when they meet a problem they’ve never seen. They don’t say “I’ve never heard of this” and fall apart. They say, calmly, “I don’t have a ready answer yet, but I will engineer the solution.”

## Engineer the solution

That’s the third step: engineer the solution.

You show that you can attack the problem from angle A, angle B and angle C. You might not know the answer at the start, but you know how to make sense of something you don’t understand yet, and how to move forward step by step.

You build this skill through practice. You get stuck many times and fix many “broken cars”, and slowly you grow your own problem-solving framework. Think of an experienced mechanic. People trust them because they have surely fixed cars in worse shape than yours. Data scientists are the same. The more mess you’ve dealt with, the better you know where to start.

## A senior’s day

So what does a senior data scientist actually do all day?

Here’s a number that surprises many people: textbook data science work, meaning building, training and evaluating models, may take only 20% to 30% of your time.

Look at it the other way round. The other 70% to 80% is communicating, understanding the problem and defining it. And that is exactly the part that is hardest to automate. I see that as good news. It’s a senior data scientist’s moat.

I felt this strongly when I worked on credit scoring, and on fraud and anomaly detection. You can’t sit alone at your computer and work out what fraud is, what counts as abnormal behaviour, or how risk is actually happening right now. You have to sit down with different teams and talk it through. How do we define it? How do we label the data? Which data should we look at? When new data comes in, what new story is it telling?

One person adds a point, someone else adds another, and you hear angles you hadn’t seen. That’s how your understanding of the domain slowly grows. So a big part of being a senior data scientist is daring to ask questions, being willing to talk to real people, and being willing to dig into domain knowledge.

## Data infrastructure

Another important part of the work is data infrastructure.

It sounds technical and complicated, but there is a simple way to picture it. It’s like tidying a home: putting things back where they belong. And you don’t tidy once and finish. Every few days you have to put things back again. Data is the same. It needs cleaning, structuring, sorting and storing in the right place, so people can find it and the company can rely on it.

I want to be clear about one thing. AI can already do the tidying. It cleans data and writes code, and it’s fast.

But what counts as “the right place”, why we sort things this way this time, and why that’s different from last time can’t be written down as a fixed rule. These are judgment calls that keep changing with the business. AI can move things around, but you are still the one who decides how they should be moved. That’s the difference between doing the work and making the call.

## Judgment and accountability

Back to the question that made me uneasy at the start.

Agents are getting closer to asking questions, breaking problems down and reasoning. They can also run models for me, write code and summarise results. I have to be honest and admit that they are climbing.

So my answer is that I need to stand where they can’t reach yet. That place is human judgment.

You are responsible for every step that AI or automation produces. You can’t just say “AI gave me this result” or “that’s what the model came up with”. You need to know why you chose this model. What the data looked like before it went into the model. What each feature represents. How to read the output. And whether it answers the question we really wanted to ask.

An example. I used to use XGBoost a lot for classification. You won’t remember every formula every day, because knowledge fades. But I keep reminding myself that I’m responsible for every step the model takes. Why did it put this row on this side? Because it’s a tree-based model. It keeps splitting the data, looking for rules that separate the classes better. You can explain that with maths, or in plain words: it’s like trying different ways to group a basket of data again and again, and moving in whichever direction makes the result clearer.

Making complex things simple is also part of a senior’s job. Being able to do the work isn’t enough. You have to really understand it.

## Where I want to stand

So where does a senior data scientist stand in the AI era? I think it’s being the person who can understand the problem, build trust, engineer the solution, judge whether the result makes sense, and explain complex things clearly. Racing AI to write code faster isn’t the point.

But I want to be honest and not end on something too comfortable. This place isn’t handed to you. AI will eat the repetitive work underneath, layer by layer. If you don’t keep climbing towards judgment, communication and domain knowledge, the moat will wash away. I don’t think AI will replace mature judgment, but I do think it will push all of us to become mature faster.
