## The interview

I recently watched an interview with Shunyu Yao, a Senior Staff Research Scientist at Google DeepMind. Something he said stayed with me, so I wanted to write it down.

Shunyu studied theoretical physics at Tsinghua University and used to work at Anthropic. In September 2025 he publicly disagreed with some of the company’s positions and resigned. Just ten days later he joined Google DeepMind’s core Gemini team. Within five months he led the development of Gemini 3 Deep Think, a reasoning model that scored 84.6% on ARC-AGI-2 and ranked among the top 8 competitive programmers in the world on Codeforces. A theoretical physicist doing frontier AI research is itself a living answer to the question of what kinds of minds do well when the tools change.

In the interview he said something that made me stop. In my words:

> The old developer model was that someone tells you what to build, and you build it. AI is replacing that at scale. What exactly replaces it, we can’t fully predict yet.

I want to build on what he said with my own view, as someone who works in data science and uses these tools every day.

## His three points

Shunyu said technical workers who do well in the AI era need three things.

**Strong enough technical foundations.** If your skills are too shallow, AI simply replaces you. You don’t need to know everything. You need real depth in at least one area, deep enough that you can check and challenge what AI gives you, instead of accepting a black box.

**Knowing what your work means for the business.** Knowing “I’m training a model” isn’t enough. You need to know what problem the model solves, which decisions it affects and what a failure would really cost. That understanding is what separates someone who carries out tasks from someone who makes decisions.

**Breaking big problems into steps.** The engineer of the future builds less and orchestrates more. They take a vague, ambitious goal and split it into clear subtasks that AI agents can carry out in order. Planning is the core skill in that role.

All three point the same way: from executor to decision-maker.

## Three more I’d add

I agree with all three. But from working with AI tools every day, I think three more things matter just as much, maybe more in practice.

### Domain knowledge

When you work with AI, you quickly find that whether you can tell if it’s on point depends on how well you know the area yourself.

That’s more than knowing the business. It’s a specific sense: noticing when AI is going round in circles without landing, or when something sounds plausible but isn’t quite right. That sense comes from a lot of domain knowledge built up over time.

Think of it as having your own map. Without one, everything AI says looks reasonable. With a map, AI speeds you up. Without one, AI is a maze, and a guide you can’t check.

### Judgment

AI is very good at optimising inside a known space. It’s not good at judgment. Is this direction worth taking? Is this the right problem to solve? If we follow this path to the end, is that what we actually wanted?

Those calls always need a person.

I think of judgment as the ability to make decisions under uncertainty. The stronger your judgment, the more you steer AI, and the less it steers you. Judgment comes from experience, from domain knowledge, and from being willing to take responsibility for a call when the outcome isn’t certain.

### Good taste

This is the point I most want to make, and the one people most often overlook.

Once I asked AI to write code in a certain style, and I linked an example I really admired. Behind that simple request were years of reading good code, using well-designed products, and building a feel for what is clean and what is over-engineered, what is truly elegant and what is only clever on the surface.

AI produces what is statistically average: lots of output that is plausible and reasonable. But there is a gap between reasonable and good. Without taste, AI just helps you produce mediocre work faster and in bigger amounts.

Good taste isn’t something you’re born with. You build it by reading widely, using excellent products, seeing great work and paying attention on purpose over a long time. AI can’t do that for you.

## Asking good questions

If I add a fourth, it’s this: the quality of your questions sets the quality of AI’s answers.

That sounds simple. But turning a vague problem into something AI can actually work with is a skill you have to practise on purpose. It needs domain knowledge, to know where the edges of the problem are. It needs judgment, to know which direction is worth asking about. And it needs taste, to recognise a good answer when you see one.

So I think the scarcest skill from here on won’t be knowing how to use AI. It will be knowing what to ask it.

## Where this leaves me

Shunyu once compared AI research today to thermodynamics in the 17th century. Back then people didn’t even know what heat was, yet Boyle’s systematic experiments produced laws that lasted and eventually led to the steam engine. We’re in a similar moment. The tools change fast and a lot is still unsettled, but the direction is getting clearer.

If I had to sum this up, I’d say the technical workers who do well in the AI era won’t be the ones most fluent with AI tools. They’ll be the ones clearest about what they’re building, why it matters and what good looks like.

These six skills (technical depth, business understanding, planning, domain knowledge, judgment and good taste) aren’t a checklist. They describe the same person from different angles: someone who knows what they’re doing. I think that is harder to replace than any tool.
